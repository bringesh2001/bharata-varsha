## Why this question is hard

The Rigveda, the oldest Vedic text, was passed down by memory for centuries before it was
written. It does not describe a journey, and it cannot easily be dated. The Harappan cities,
on the other hand, left plenty of archaeology but a script nobody can read. So historians are
trying to connect two kinds of evidence that do not speak to each other directly
<Cite id="bryant-2001" />.

## What changed recently

Until the 2010s the debate rested mainly on language and archaeology. Since then, DNA from
ancient skeletons has added a third line of evidence. A large study published in 2019 analysed
hundreds of ancient individuals from South and Central Asia <Cite id="narasimhan-2019" />.
Popular accounts by a geneticist and by a journalist explain what such studies can and cannot
show <Cite id="reich-2018" /> <Cite id="joseph-2018" />.

DNA shows ancestry, not language. Linking a genetic signal to the arrival of a language is
itself an argument, and critics of the migration view point this out
<Cite id="bryant-2001" />.

## Why it is argued so fiercely

The question has been tied to politics since the colonial period, when "Aryan" ideas were used
to rank peoples. Today it is often argued in terms of who is "native" to India. Keeping the
historical evidence separate from those present-day uses is the first step to reading the
debate fairly <Cite id="bryant-2001" />.
