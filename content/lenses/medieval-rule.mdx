## Two kinds of evidence

Persian chronicles, written to glorify rulers, often describe conquest in religious language,
including the destruction of temples. One side reads these as reliable accounts. The other
argues that chronicles exaggerate for effect, and compares them with inscriptions, buildings and
records <Cite id="sarkar-1912" /> <Cite id="eaton-2000" />.

## Counting temples

Sita Ram Goel and colleagues compiled long lists of temples said to have been destroyed or
converted <Cite id="goel-1990" />. Richard Eaton, counting only cases he could document, found
far fewer, and argued that most were attacks on the royal temples of defeated or rebellious
rulers <Cite id="eaton-2000" />. Both sides agree that the Quwwat al-Islam mosque and others
reused material from temples.

## Aurangzeb

The debate is sharpest over Aurangzeb, who reimposed the jizya and ordered some temples
destroyed, while also employing many Hindu nobles and protecting other temples. Historians weigh
these facts very differently <Cite id="sarkar-1912" /> <Cite id="truschke-2017" />
<Cite id="richards-1993" />.
