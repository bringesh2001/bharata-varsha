Long before the Vedas or the Buddha, people in what is now Pakistan and north-western India
built some of the world's first cities. We call their civilisation the Indus or Harappan
civilisation, after the river and after Harappa, the first city to be excavated. Because their
writing cannot be read, almost everything we know comes from archaeology.

<Callout kind="note" title="Three phases">
  Archaeologists usually divide the story into an Early Harappan phase (about 3300–2600 BCE),
  the Mature Harappan phase of the big cities (about 2600–1900 BCE), and a Late Harappan phase
  (about 1900–1300 BCE) when the cities emptied. All these dates are approximate
  <Cite id="possehl-2002" />.
</Callout>

## What happened

**Rediscovery.** The cities were forgotten for thousands of years. Excavations at Harappa in
1921 and Mohenjo-daro in 1922 revealed that both belonged to the same, previously unknown
civilisation, which John Marshall announced to the world in 1924 <Cite id="singh-2008" />.

**Village to city.** The cities did not appear from nowhere. Farming villages in the region go
back thousands of years, and in the Early Harappan phase towns grew larger and began to share
styles of pottery, seals and building <Cite id="kenoyer-1998" />.

**The age of cities.** Around 2600 BCE, large planned cities appear: Harappa and Mohenjo-daro
on the Indus system, Ganweriwala and Rakhigarhi to the east, Dholavira in Gujarat, and the port
town of Lothal. Well over a thousand Harappan sites are now known, spread over an area larger
than either ancient Egypt or Mesopotamia <Cite id="possehl-2002" /> <Cite id="wright-2010" />.

**The cities empty.** After about 1900 BCE, the big cities shrank and were gradually
abandoned. People did not vanish: many moved into smaller villages, especially further east,
and the shared city culture broke up into regional ones <Cite id="possehl-2002" />.

## How it was run

This is one of the great puzzles of the Indus world. Mesopotamia and Egypt left palaces, royal
tombs and images of kings. The Indus cities have none that archaeologists can clearly identify
<Cite id="kenoyer-1998" />.

Yet something kept the cities remarkably consistent over a huge area. Bricks were made in the
same proportions (1:2:4) from Gujarat to Punjab, and stone weights followed one standard system
<Cite id="kenoyer-1998" />. Historians suggest several answers: rule by groups of merchants or
elites rather than one king, a powerful religious class, or a network of city-states that shared
customs. None is proven <Cite id="wright-2010" />.

## How people lived

**Cities.** Mohenjo-daro was laid out with main streets and smaller lanes, raised mounds for
some public buildings, and houses of baked brick, many with their own wells and bathrooms.
Covered drains ran along the streets, a level of public sanitation unusual for its time
<Cite id="kenoyer-1998" />. The Great Bath, a large watertight tank sealed with bitumen, may
have been used for ritual bathing <Cite id="mcintosh-2008" />.

**Farming.** The cities rested on farming: wheat and barley, pulses, sesame, and cotton, along
with cattle, sheep and goats <Cite id="wright-2010" />.

**Crafts and trade.** Craft workers made long carnelian beads, shell bangles, faience, copper
and bronze tools, and the famous square stone seals. Indus goods reached the Persian Gulf and
Mesopotamia, where texts name a land called Meluhha that most scholars identify with the Indus
region <Cite id="ratnagar-2004" />. See [India & Mesopotamia](/connections/india-and-mesopotamia).

## Ideas and faith

We know little for certain about Indus beliefs, because there are no readable texts. Figurines,
seal images and buildings like the Great Bath hint at ritual life. Older books often read later
Hindu gods, especially an early "Shiva", into some seal images; many archaeologists now treat
such readings as possible but unproven <Cite id="mcintosh-2008" />.

## What evidence do we have?

<Callout kind="evidence" title="Archaeology: strong evidence">
  City plans, houses, drains, crafts, weights and trade goods are well documented from a
  century of excavation <Cite id="kenoyer-1998" /> <Cite id="possehl-2002" />.
</Callout>

- **Excavated cities and villages**, and surveys of hundreds of smaller sites.
- **Seals and sealings** with short inscriptions and animal images.
- **Mesopotamian texts** that mention Meluhha and its goods <Cite id="ratnagar-2004" />.
- **Ancient DNA.** In 2019, a genome from a woman buried at Rakhigarhi was published. It showed
  no detectable ancestry from steppe pastoralists <Cite id="shinde-2019" />. See the debate on
  [the Vedic people](/lenses/vedic-origins).

## What remains debated?

<Callout kind="debate" title="Can the Indus script be read?">
  The signs appear mostly in very short inscriptions, often around five signs long. Asko Parpola
  has argued that they write an early Dravidian language <Cite id="parpola-1994" />. Others have
  argued that the signs were not a full writing system at all, a view many scholars reject
  <Cite id="farmer-2004" />. Without a long bilingual text, no reading has been accepted.
</Callout>

- **Who ruled the cities?** See above: there is no agreed answer.
- **Why did the cities decline?** Most scholars now point to several causes together: a
  weakening monsoon, rivers changing course or drying up (including the Ghaggar-Hakra), and the
  breakdown of long-distance trade. The older idea of a violent "Aryan invasion" destroying the
  cities is no longer accepted <Cite id="possehl-2002" /> <Cite id="wright-2010" />.
- **How does it connect to later India?** Some crafts, farming practices and perhaps symbols
  continue into later periods, but how much is debated <Cite id="singh-2008" />.
