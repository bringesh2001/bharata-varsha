Around 321 BCE, a young ruler named Chandragupta took the throne of Magadha, a kingdom in the
middle Ganga plain. Within a few generations, his family ruled an area larger than any
before it: from Afghanistan in the northwest to Bengal in the east, and deep into the Deccan
in the south. This page explains how that happened, how the empire worked, how people lived
under it, and how we know any of this at all.

<Callout kind="note" title="Why this era matters">
  The Mauryan period is the first time we can see a single state across most of the
  subcontinent. It is also the first period where a ruler speaks to us directly, in
  inscriptions he had carved in stone.
</Callout>

## What happened

**The rise of Chandragupta.** Before the Mauryas, Magadha was ruled by the Nanda dynasty,
which was already rich and powerful. Chandragupta overthrew the Nandas around 321 BCE and made
Pataliputra (near modern Patna) his capital <Cite id="thapar-2002" />. Later Indian tradition
gives much of the credit to his adviser Chanakya, also called Kautilya. Those stories were
written down centuries afterwards, so historians treat the details with care
<Cite id="singh-2008" />.

**Meeting the Greeks.** Alexander of Macedon had reached the Indus region a few years
earlier, in 326–325 BCE. After his death, his general Seleucus tried to hold the eastern
lands. Around 303 BCE, Seleucus and Chandragupta made a treaty. Seleucus gave up territory
in what is now Afghanistan and Pakistan, and received war elephants in return. Greek writers
called the Indian king *Sandrokottos* <Cite id="mccrindle-1877" />.

**An ambassador at court.** Seleucus sent an envoy, Megasthenes, to Pataliputra. He wrote a
book about India called the *Indica*. The book itself is lost. What survives are passages
quoted by later Greek and Roman writers <Cite id="mccrindle-1877" />.

**Bindusara.** Chandragupta's son Bindusara ruled next, from about 297 BCE. Very little is
known about his reign. Jain tradition says Chandragupta gave up the throne, became a Jain
monk and died in the south, at Shravanabelagola in present-day Karnataka. This is a later
tradition, not a contemporary record <Cite id="thapar-2002" />.

**Ashoka and the Kalinga war.** Ashoka, Bindusara's son, came to the throne around 268 BCE.
In the eighth year after his consecration he conquered Kalinga, on the east coast (roughly
modern Odisha). One of his own inscriptions, Major Rock Edict XIII, describes the war. It
says 150,000 people were carried away, 100,000 were killed, and many more died. It then says
the king felt deep regret, and that from then on he cared above all for *dhamma*
<Cite id="hultzsch-1925" />.

<Callout kind="evidence" title="A detail worth noticing">
  Edict XIII, the one that expresses regret for the war, was not carved at the two major
  inscription sites inside Kalinga itself, Dhauli and Jaugada. Those sites have two separate
  edicts addressed to local officials instead. Historians read this in different ways, but it
  is a reminder that the edicts were written for particular audiences
  <Cite id="thapar-1961" />.
</Callout>

## How the empire was run

The Mauryan kings ruled from Pataliputra. Excavations there have found remains of a long
timber wall and a large pillared hall <Cite id="singh-2008" />. Megasthenes also wrote about
a city with wooden walls <Cite id="mccrindle-1877" />.

Ashoka's edicts name several kinds of officials. In the edicts, he sends officers on regular
tours of their regions, and he creates a new office, the *dhamma-mahamattas*, whose job was
to spread and supervise dhamma among all groups of people <Cite id="hultzsch-1925" />.

Many older books describe the Mauryan state using the *Arthashastra*, a long Sanskrit text on
statecraft traditionally said to be by Kautilya. It sets out ministers, spies, taxes and
superintendents for almost everything. But the version we have was probably put together over
several centuries, and Patrick Olivelle dates it well after the Mauryan period
<Cite id="olivelle-2013" />. So it tells us how some later thinkers imagined a strong kingdom
should work. It is not a direct description of Chandragupta's government.

## How people lived

Most people were farmers. The fertile Ganga plain grew rice and other crops, and land tax was
a major source of royal income <Cite id="singh-2008" />. One of Ashoka's inscriptions, at
Lumbini, says he reduced the tax owed by the village there because it was the Buddha's
birthplace <Cite id="hultzsch-1925" />.

Towns and trade grew. Silver punch-marked coins, which had begun before the Mauryas, were in
wide use. Roads connected the capital with the northwest and the Deccan
<Cite id="thapar-2002" />.

Megasthenes described Indian society as divided into seven groups, such as farmers,
soldiers and officials. This does not match Indian descriptions of social order, and
historians think he was fitting what he saw into Greek categories <Cite id="mccrindle-1877" />
<Cite id="thapar-2002" />.

The edicts also show the people at the edges of the empire. Edict XIII has a stern message
for the forest peoples, reminding them of the king's power even as it speaks of his remorse
<Cite id="hultzsch-1925" />.

## Ideas and faith

Ashoka calls himself a lay follower of the Buddha in one of his minor rock edicts
<Cite id="hultzsch-1925" />. But the *dhamma* he promoted in the edicts was not simply
Buddhist doctrine. It asked people to:

- respect parents, elders and teachers
- treat servants and slaves decently
- be generous to Brahmins and to ascetics of all sects
- avoid needless killing of animals
- hold back from praising their own sect and attacking others'

Romila Thapar argued that dhamma was a social ethic meant to hold a very varied empire
together <Cite id="thapar-1961" />. Other historians put more weight on Ashoka's personal
Buddhist faith <Cite id="lahiri-2015" />.

Several religious groups flourished side by side. Ashoka gave caves in the Barabar hills
to the Ajivikas, a group of ascetics whose texts are now lost <Cite id="hultzsch-1925" />.

## Art and building

The best-known Mauryan works are Ashoka's stone pillars. Each is a single shaft of polished
sandstone, topped by an animal capital: a lion, a bull or an elephant. The lion capital from
Sarnath, with four lions back to back, was adopted as India's national emblem in 1950
<Cite id="singh-2008" />.

Buddhist tradition credits Ashoka with building many stupas. The brick core of the Great
Stupa at Sanchi is thought to date from his time <Cite id="singh-2008" />.

## What evidence do we have?

<Callout kind="evidence" title="Ashoka's inscriptions: strong evidence">
  More than thirty inscription sites survive across South Asia. They are the closest thing we
  have to the voice of the king himself <Cite id="hultzsch-1925" />.
</Callout>

- **Inscriptions.** Most edicts are in Prakrit, written in the Brahmi script. In the
  northwest some are in Kharoshthi, and at Kandahar in Afghanistan there are versions in Greek
  and Aramaic. James Prinsep deciphered Brahmi in 1837. The edicts call the king *Devanampiya
  Piyadasi*, "beloved of the gods". The name Ashoka appears in an edict found at Maski in 1915,
  which confirmed who Piyadasi was <Cite id="singh-2008" /> <Cite id="lahiri-2015" />.
- **Greek and Roman writers.** Fragments of Megasthenes and later authors who used his work
  <Cite id="mccrindle-1877" />.
- **Indian texts.** The *Arthashastra* (see above), Buddhist chronicles from Sri Lanka, Jain
  texts and the Puranas. Most were written down long after the events
  <Cite id="thapar-2002" />.
- **Archaeology and coins.** Excavations at Pataliputra and other cities, pillars and
  capitals, and punch-marked coins <Cite id="singh-2008" />.

## What remains debated?

<Callout kind="debate" title="How centralised was the empire?">
  Earlier work, including Thapar's own 1961 book, pictured a strongly centralised state. In
  later lectures, Thapar argued instead for a capital region, core areas, and outer regions
  that were ruled much more loosely <Cite id="thapar-1987" />.
</Callout>

- **When exactly?** The start of Chandragupta's reign and the dates of Ashoka's reign are
  worked out from several sources that do not fully agree. Dates on this page are
  approximate.
- **What was dhamma?** A personal Buddhist mission, a political ethic, or both
  <Cite id="thapar-1961" /> <Cite id="lahiri-2015" />?
- **Was Ashoka a pacifist?** He renounced conquest, but he kept his army, warned the forest
  peoples, and did not abolish the death penalty; one pillar edict gives condemned prisoners
  a three-day delay <Cite id="hultzsch-1925" />.
- **How old is the Arthashastra?** See Olivelle's introduction for the case that it is later
  than the Mauryas <Cite id="olivelle-2013" />.

## How it ended

After Ashoka's death around 232 BCE, his successors ruled for shorter periods and the empire
seems to have shrunk. Around 185 BCE, the last Mauryan king, Brihadratha, was killed by his
general Pushyamitra, who founded the Shunga dynasty <Cite id="thapar-1961" />. Why the empire
declined is debated. Explanations range from weak successors to the costs of running such a
large state <Cite id="thapar-1961" /> <Cite id="thapar-1987" />.
