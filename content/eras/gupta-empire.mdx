After the Kushans and Satavahanas, northern India was divided among many kingdoms. In the
early 300s CE, a family from the middle Ganga valley, the Guptas, began to build a new empire.
This page covers how they rose, how they ruled, how people lived, and why the famous "golden
age" label is now debated.

## What happened

**Founding.** Chandragupta I is counted as the first great Gupta king. A new calendar era, the
Gupta era, begins in 319–320 CE, probably from his accession. His coins show him with his queen
Kumaradevi of the Licchavi clan, which suggests the marriage mattered politically
<Cite id="agrawal-1989" /> <Cite id="singh-2008" />.

**Samudragupta's campaigns.** His son Samudragupta is known mainly from a long praise poem
carved on a pillar now at Allahabad (Prayagraj). It lists kings he defeated in the north, and
southern kings he captured and then released, as well as rulers on the frontiers who paid
him tribute <Cite id="fleet-1888" />. It is a court poem, so historians read its boasts with
care <Cite id="thapar-2002" />.

**Chandragupta II.** Around 400 CE, Chandragupta II defeated the Western Kshatrapas, extending
Gupta power to Gujarat and the western sea coast. His daughter Prabhavatigupta married into the
Vakataka dynasty of the Deccan, linking the two powers <Cite id="singh-2008" />.

**A pilgrim's view.** The Chinese Buddhist monk Faxian travelled through India in the early
400s. He describes prosperous towns, Buddhist monasteries, rest houses and hospitals, light
punishments, and people called Chandalas who had to live apart and announce their approach
<Cite id="legge-1886" />.

**Pressure and decline.** Skandagupta fought off invaders called the Hunas in the mid-400s.
Later Huna attacks, the rise of powerful local rulers, and disputed successions weakened the
dynasty, and by about 550 CE the Gupta empire had broken up <Cite id="agrawal-1989" />
<Cite id="singh-2008" />.

## How it was run

The Guptas ruled the Ganga valley directly. Further away, many rulers kept their own lands and
acknowledged Gupta overlordship, so the empire was looser than the Mauryan state
<Cite id="thapar-2002" />. Kings issued gold coins in many designs, showing them as archers,
horsemen or musicians <Cite id="singh-2008" />.

Inscriptions record growing numbers of land grants, often to Brahmins and temples, sometimes
with rights to collect revenue. Some historians see this as a shift of power to local landholders
<Cite id="thapar-2002" />.

## How people lived

Most people farmed. Towns and long-distance trade continued, though some historians think
trade with the Roman world had declined from its earlier peak <Cite id="singh-2008" />.

Texts and Faxian's account suggest a society organised more strictly by caste, with groups
like the Chandalas treated as untouchable <Cite id="legge-1886" /> <Cite id="thapar-2002" />.

## Ideas, science and art

**Mathematics and astronomy.** In 499 CE, Aryabhata wrote the *Aryabhatiya*, which includes a
table of sines and methods for astronomical calculation <Cite id="clark-1930" />
<Cite id="plofker-2009" />. See [Inventions & ideas](/ideas).

**Literature.** Kalidasa, the most celebrated Sanskrit poet and playwright, is usually placed
in this period, though his exact dates are uncertain <Cite id="singh-2008" />.

**Temples and caves.** Some of the earliest surviving free-standing stone temples in India date
from this time, along with carved caves such as those at Udayagiri. The great painted caves at
Ajanta were made in the same century, under the Vakataka king Harishena rather than the Guptas
<Cite id="spink-2005" />.

**Metalwork.** The iron pillar now in the Qutb complex, Delhi, bears an inscription of a king
Chandra, usually identified with Chandragupta II, and is famous for resisting rust for more than
1,500 years <Cite id="balasubramaniam-2002" />.

## What evidence do we have?

<Callout kind="evidence" title="Inscriptions and coins: strong evidence">
  Gupta history rests mainly on dated inscriptions and thousands of coins, which give names,
  dates and titles <Cite id="fleet-1888" /> <Cite id="agrawal-1989" />.
</Callout>

- **Inscriptions** on pillars, copper plates and temples <Cite id="fleet-1888" />.
- **Coins**, especially gold dinaras <Cite id="singh-2008" />.
- **Travellers' accounts**, above all Faxian's <Cite id="legge-1886" />.
- **Literature, science and buildings** from the period.

## What remains debated?

<Callout kind="debate" title="Was it really a 'golden age'?">
  Historians writing in the late 1800s and early 1900s, including Indian nationalists, called
  the Gupta period a classical or golden age. Later historians point out that the label fits
  court culture far better than the lives of most people, and that caste restrictions hardened in
  this period <Cite id="thapar-2002" />.
</Callout>

- **How big was the empire?** Court poems like the Allahabad inscription may overstate the
  king's reach <Cite id="thapar-2002" />.
- **Who ruled when?** The order and dates of some later kings are still disputed
  <Cite id="agrawal-1989" />.
- **What did land grants change?** Whether they weakened the central state is argued
  <Cite id="thapar-2002" />.
