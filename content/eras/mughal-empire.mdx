In 1526 a Central Asian prince named Babur defeated the Sultan of Delhi and founded a dynasty
that would rule for over three hundred years. The Mughals governed tens of millions of people,
built the Taj Mahal, and shaped Indian languages, painting, food and government. This page
covers their rise, how their state worked, and the debates about why it weakened.

## What happened

**Babur (1526–1530).** A descendant of Timur and Chinggis Khan, Babur ruled Kabul before
turning to India. At Panipat in 1526 he defeated Ibrahim Lodi, the last Delhi sultan, using
field guns and mobile cavalry, and the next year he defeated a Rajput alliance led by Rana Sanga.
His memoir, the *Baburnama*, is unusually personal and frank <Cite id="thackston-1996" />.

**Humayun and Sher Shah.** Babur's son Humayun lost the empire to the Afghan ruler Sher Shah
Sur in 1540 and spent fifteen years in exile. Sher Shah's short reign left lasting reforms in
coinage, roads and revenue. Humayun regained Delhi in 1555 but died the next year
<Cite id="richards-1993" />.

**Akbar (1556–1605).** Akbar came to the throne at thirteen and expanded the empire across the
north, into Gujarat, Bengal and the edge of the Deccan. He built alliances with Rajput rulers,
abolished the *jizya* tax on non-Muslims in 1564, and held debates among scholars of many
religions. His idea of *sulh-i kul*, "peace with all", shaped his policy
<Cite id="richards-1993" /> <Cite id="blochmann-1873" />.

**Jahangir and Shah Jahan.** Under Jahangir (1605–1627) and Shah Jahan (1628–1658), the court
reached its height of wealth and display. Shah Jahan built the Taj Mahal at Agra as a tomb for
his wife Mumtaz Mahal, and a new capital at Delhi, Shahjahanabad <Cite id="koch-2006" />.

**Aurangzeb (1658–1707).** Aurangzeb won a war of succession against his brothers and deposed
his father. He conquered the Deccan sultanates of Bijapur and Golconda in the 1680s, bringing
the empire to its greatest size, but spent his last decades fighting the Marathas
<Cite id="richards-1993" /> <Cite id="gordon-1993" />.

**After 1707.** Under weaker successors, governors in Hyderabad, Bengal and Awadh became
independent in all but name, and the Marathas became the strongest power in India. The Persian
ruler Nadir Shah sacked Delhi in 1739. The emperor kept great prestige but little power, and
after the revolt of 1857 the British exiled the last emperor, Bahadur Shah Zafar
<Cite id="richards-1993" />.

## How it was run

**Mansabdars.** Akbar organised the nobility into ranks called *mansabs*. Each rank set a
noble's pay and the number of horsemen he had to supply. Nobles were usually paid through the
revenue of assigned lands (*jagirs*), which were moved regularly so that no one put down local
roots <Cite id="richards-1993" />.

**Land revenue.** The state's wealth came mainly from a tax on harvests. Under Akbar's minister
Todar Mal, land was measured and the tax was set from average yields and prices
<Cite id="blochmann-1873" /> <Cite id="habib-1963" />.

## How people lived

Most people were peasants, paying a large share of their harvest in tax. Towns and crafts
flourished: India exported cotton textiles across Asia and to Europe, and silver flowed in to
pay for them <Cite id="richards-1993" />.

Persian was the language of government, while Hindavi, later called Hindustani or
Urdu-Hindi, grew as a shared spoken language. Court painting blended Persian, Indian and, later,
European styles <Cite id="richards-1993" />.

## What evidence do we have?

<Callout kind="evidence" title="Rich written records">
  Compared with earlier periods, the Mughal age is very well documented: memoirs, official
  histories, revenue records, letters, European travellers' accounts and buildings
  <Cite id="richards-1993" />.
</Callout>

- **Memoirs and court histories**, such as the *Baburnama* and Abu'l Fazl's *Akbarnama* and
  *Ain-i Akbari* <Cite id="thackston-1996" /> <Cite id="blochmann-1873" />.
- **Revenue and administrative documents**, used by historians to study the economy
  <Cite id="habib-1963" />.
- **Buildings, paintings and coins.**

## What remains debated?

<Callout kind="debate" title="Why did the empire weaken after 1707?">
  Irfan Habib argued that heavy revenue demands pushed peasants into revolt and caused an
  agrarian crisis <Cite id="habib-1963" />. Later historians argue that the eighteenth century
  was less a collapse than a shift of power to strong regional states <Cite id="alam-subrahmanyam-1998" />.
</Callout>

- **Aurangzeb's reign.** Was he a religious zealot who broke Akbar's settlement, or a ruler
  whose policies, including temple destruction and the return of the jizya in 1679, are better
  explained by politics? This is one of the most heated debates in Indian history today
  <Cite id="truschke-2017" /> <Cite id="richards-1993" />.
- **Conquest or composite culture?** How to describe the place of Muslim rulers in Indian
  history is argued in the present as much as about the past.
- **The Marathas.** Were they rebels, regional state-builders, or an Indian alternative to the
  Mughals? <Cite id="gordon-1993" />
