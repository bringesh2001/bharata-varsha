The British did not arrive as conquerors. The English East India Company came to trade, and
for a century and a half it was one foreign company among many. How it became the ruler of a
subcontinent, how its rule changed India, and how Indians fought to end it, is the story of this
era.

## What happened

**From trade to rule (1757–1818).** In 1757, at Plassey in Bengal, Company forces under Robert
Clive defeated the Nawab of Bengal, helped by defections in the Nawab's own camp. After a further
victory at Buxar in 1764, the Mughal emperor granted the Company the right to collect Bengal's
revenue in 1765 <Cite id="bandyopadhyay-2004" />. Wars against Mysore, the Marathas and later
the Sikhs brought most of India under Company control or alliance by 1849
<Cite id="bayly-1988" />.

**The revolt of 1857.** In May 1857, Indian soldiers of the Company's army rose at Meerut and
marched to Delhi, proclaiming the old Mughal emperor as their leader. The revolt spread across
north and central India, drawing in peasants, landholders and rulers such as the Rani of Jhansi.
It was put down with great violence by 1858 <Cite id="mukherjee-1984" />
<Cite id="bandyopadhyay-2004" />.

**The Raj (1858–1947).** The British Crown took over from the Company in 1858. India was ruled
by a Viceroy, a small British civil service, and alliances with hundreds of princely states
<Cite id="bandyopadhyay-2004" />.

**The national movement.** The Indian National Congress was founded in 1885. After the
partition of Bengal in 1905, the Swadeshi movement called for boycotting British goods. M. K.
Gandhi returned from South Africa in 1915 and led mass campaigns of non-violent resistance: the
Non-Cooperation movement (1920–22), the Salt March (1930) and Quit India (1942). Others chose
different paths, from revolutionaries to Subhas Chandra Bose's Indian National Army
<Cite id="chandra-1988" /> <Cite id="sarkar-1983" />.

**Independence and Partition.** On 15 August 1947 India became independent, and British India
was partitioned into India and Pakistan. Partition was accompanied by mass violence and one of
the largest migrations in history <Cite id="bandyopadhyay-2004" />. See
[Independent India](/eras/independent-india).

## How it was run

**Land revenue.** As under the Mughals, tax on land was the state's main income. In Bengal, the
Permanent Settlement of 1793 fixed the revenue owed by landholders (*zamindars*); in much of
the south and west, the state dealt directly with cultivators <Cite id="bandyopadhyay-2004" />.

**Army and civil service.** British rule rested on a large army, mostly of Indian soldiers, and a
small, mainly British, Indian Civil Service. Indians entered higher posts only slowly
<Cite id="sarkar-1983" />.

**Railways, telegraph and law.** Railways spread from the 1850s, alongside the telegraph, new
courts and codified law <Cite id="roy-2000" />.

## How people lived

Most Indians remained farmers. Commercial crops such as cotton, indigo, jute and opium grew in
importance, and hand-weaving declined as factory cloth arrived from Britain
<Cite id="roy-2000" />. Severe famines struck repeatedly, especially in the 1870s and 1890s,
and how far British policy caused or worsened them is still argued <Cite id="davis-2001" />
<Cite id="roy-2000" />.

The period also saw new schools and universities, a printing boom in Indian languages, and
reform movements: on the status of women, on caste, and on religion <Cite id="bandyopadhyay-2004" />.

## What evidence do we have?

<Callout kind="evidence" title="An enormous archive">
  Government records, censuses, newspapers, letters, petitions, memoirs and photographs survive
  in huge numbers. The challenge is less a lack of sources than whose voices they record
  <Cite id="guha-1983" />.
</Callout>

- **Official records**, which tell the story from the rulers' side.
- **Indian writings**: newspapers, pamphlets, speeches, and books such as Naoroji's on Indian
  poverty and Gandhi's *Hind Swaraj* <Cite id="naoroji-1901" /> <Cite id="gandhi-1909" />.
- **History from below**: historians of peasant revolts read official records "against the
  grain" to recover the views of people who left few writings <Cite id="guha-1983" />.

## What remains debated?

<Callout kind="debate" title="1857: a mutiny or a war of independence?">
  British writers called it the Indian Mutiny. In 1909 V. D. Savarkar called it the first Indian
  war of independence <Cite id="savarkar-1909" />. Later historians show that it was more than a
  soldiers' mutiny, with wide popular support in places like Awadh, but also that it was not a
  single national movement <Cite id="mukherjee-1984" />.
</Callout>

- **Did British rule drain India's wealth, or modernise it?** See the debate
  [British rule: modernisation or a drain of wealth?](/lenses/drain-of-wealth).
- **How should the national movement be told?** As the story of Congress and its leaders, or
  also of peasants, workers, women, Dalits and regional movements? <Cite id="sarkar-1983" />
  <Cite id="guha-1983" />
- **Why did Partition happen?** Historians still debate the roles of the British, the Congress
  and the Muslim League <Cite id="bandyopadhyay-2004" />.
