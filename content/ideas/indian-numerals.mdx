## One system, three scripts

The digits most of the world writes today, 0 to 9, are a version of a system first used in
India. What travelled was not the shapes of the digits but the idea: nine signs and a zero, and
the rule that a digit's value depends on its place <Cite id="plofker-2009" />.

## The journey

**By about 500 CE, India.** Indian mathematicians write numbers in a decimal place-value system.
The Bakhshali manuscript, an arithmetic text found in 1881, marks zero with a dot. Its date is
debated <Cite id="plofker-2009" />.

**628 CE, India.** Brahmagupta gives rules for adding, subtracting and multiplying with zero,
treating it as a number in its own right <Cite id="colebrooke-1817" /> <Cite id="plofker-2009" />.

**c. 825 CE, Baghdad.** Al-Khwarizmi writes a book on calculating with Indian numerals. His
name later gives us the word "algorithm" <Cite id="plofker-2009" />.

**1202, Pisa.** Fibonacci, who learned the numerals in North Africa, opens his *Liber Abaci* by
teaching "the nine Indian figures" and the sign 0 <Cite id="sigler-2002" />.

## Credit where it's due

<Callout kind="evidence" title="What India did first, and what it didn't">
  The Babylonians and the Maya also used a sign for an empty place. What Indian mathematicians
  first wrote down was zero as a number with its own rules, inside a decimal place-value system
  <Cite id="plofker-2009" /> <Cite id="ifrah-2000" />.
</Callout>
